StreetEats

The convenient platform for finding and ordering from food trucks

My Role

Sole UX Designer, Researcher

Responsibilities

User research, Conducting interviews & surveys, Wireframing, Low + Hi-fi prototyping, Interface design, Conducting usability studies, Iterative design

Tools

Figma, Adobe Photoshop, Google Forms

Context

Solo project

The Initial Problem: Inconvenient food trucks at Rutgers

The famous food trucks at Rutgers are popular destinations for students, but they are very inconvenient. These food trucks are hard to locate as they move, lack comprehensive online menus, and often have huge lines that make them impossible to visit when short on time.

The Solution: StreetEats

An app that can help students get information like menus, schedules, and locations for these food trucks. Also include the ability to order online for quick and easy pick up.

User Research

Summary

I conducted interviews to understand the users I would be designing for. I found that the primary user group was students at Rutgers who want to use the food trucks between classes with limited time. The app also benefits underclassmen who have a limited knowledge about the trucks as well as where they are and what they serve.

Primary pain points

Location of trucks

There is very limited information anywhere about where the food trucks are parked each day at Rutgers, and when they will be there, especially since they move daily.

Information about food

There are no easily accessible menus online for any of the food trucks, and no dietary information is available either. The only way to see the menu is to walk up to the food truck in person.

Time spent ordering

The lines for the food trucks are always very long, requiring at least a 20 minute wait, and there is no way to order online for pick up, making quick stops impossible.

Journey map: Locate a Rutgers food truck and grab food on the go

This is the typical journey of someone who wants to order from the food trucks at Rutgers. There is no streamlined process and factors like time can vary greatly.


Goal: locate a food truck and get lunch there before class

Creating this journey map helped me pinpoint what aspects of the current food truck situation were most challenging for students and why. This allowed me to explore the most beneficial solutions to these problems.

Starting the Design: Translating insights from research

I wanted to make sure users who are unfamiliar with campus could still easily find the food trucks, so I chose a live map for the home screen to begin the user flow. I also wanted to have each truck shown in list form with basic but essential information for each.

Next, I wanted all important information for each truck to be in one central location for users to easily find all of it before ordering their food, so I laid it all out in the truck info screen.

Expanding the Scope to All Food Trucks

It was at this point that I realized there is nothing necessarily unique about Rutgers food trucks, and a much wider audience could benefit from this product. So, I decided to change focus from an app for the Rutgers food trucks to an app that could help people discover and order from food trucks anywhere, of all kinds. (Including the Rutgers trucks, of course).

Food trucks around the world and their customers have the same everyday hardships and problems that the ones at Rutgers do. Long lines, misinformation about their locations, and a lack of information about food served is common in all kinds of food trucks.

My user research at Rutgers has definitely proven insightful, as food trucks here seem to be prominently dealing with all of these issues, and being in a very popular, walkable area has only made them worse. So, using what I found with this research helped a lot with moving forward with the app.

To ensure goals of this wider audience of all food truck customers would still align with those of Rutgers students, I performed another round of user interviews with a more general demographic, as well as usability tests with my low-fidelity flows.

The Final Design

With the new scope of all food trucks, and more general usability studies, I was able to learn what would work best for this wider audience, and went through multiple design iterations.

I found that general food truck customers cared more about the food itself than college students did - and features like dietary information and in-depth meal customization were notably lacking in my designs. So, I used this input towards new iterations of my designs.

These are the final designs for the app that now helped users find and order from food trucks anywhere.

Finding food trucks

For finding food trucks to eat at, users have the option of browsing based on type of food and cuisine, as well as their locations.

The browse page lets users see trucks nearby in categories to find the food they are craving. A recommended nearby section allows quick selection of a nearby truck in a hurry.

The explore page lets users find trucks that are nearby on a map. This is a very intuitive way to explore and learn about the closest options. Since one of the main benefits of food trucks for the consumer is an eating establishment in a convenient location, this map takes full advantage of that and helps users explore what is nearby.

Learning about a truck

After selecting a truck in either of the past screens, the user is brought to the truck’s main page. Here, all important information about a truck can be seen. The hours for the day are listed, with a dropdown to see the truck’s weekly schedule. A truck can also be liked, adding it to the favorites page.

If interested in placing an order with the app, the order pickup button can be selected.

Creating an order

Once an order is started, the user is shown all the items on the menu, organized into categories. There are tabs at the top for easy browsing and jumping to different categories.

After selecting an item, the item’s information screen is shown, with a list of ingredients and caloric and allergen information, as well as the ability to customize the ingredients in the food. For those who want extra sauce, or to remove ingredients, for example, it can be done by expanding the menu and editing the ingredients.

Special instructions can then be added (ex. cook well), and then the quantity of the item. Finally, the item can be added to the order, showing the final cost of this item in the button.

Once an item is added, the floating “current order” button can be seen.

Finalizing the order

Selecting the “current order” button brings the user to their order screen. Adding more items or closing the screen is easy.

Once the user is satisfied with their order, they can press the continue button, again seeing their total cost as they do so.

A slide-up menu is revealed, confirming the food truck and cost, as well as showing the user their payment options, and an option to tip the food truck.

Order confirmed! Selecting the address of the food truck opens it in the user’s default map application. The user can then navigate to the truck to pick up their freshly made meal!

Takeaways

What was new to me?

This was my first time designing an app that involved a checkout process. There are lots of patterns and features that users subconsciously expect with an app like this, and with a process that is used so often. The flow from ordering items, to the cart, possibly back to ordering items, and checking out, had to be fluid and remain familiar so users naturally know what to expect and aren’t confused while they’re using the app.

What was the most challenging?

The scope of this project proved to be a bit of a challenge. While at first, I wanted to only focus on the food trucks at my school, Rutgers University, I realized this may not be the best route. I didn’t want to limit my ideas and make this a niche app that only students at one university could use, when there wasn’t much of a reason to limit it. It also felt like I was just designing for this as it was familiar to myself, rather than thinking for the general population who experiences problems at any food truck. So, to benefit more users and have the app be applicable to anyone, I decided to focus on all food trucks. This felt like the right decision as the design and app structure didn’t have to change much, which, I felt, showed my limits on the scope were unnecessary. Thinking from the user’s perspective, it also seemed logical to be able to use this app for all food trucks.

How does my solution help users?

StreetEats helps users by finding food trucks nearby, and making ordering from them seamless. I wanted this app to lean in to what makes food trucks special - their convenient locations and unique menus. By offering a quick and easy way to find food trucks on the go, and order ahead of time, users can take advantage of these factors that make food trucks unique, and can quickly grab food on the go.

How would I measure success?

Success for StreetEats would mean more business for food trucks. Not all food trucks have a website that makes them easy to learn about, or any kind of presence on Google Maps, etc. There is no easy way to explore food trucks around, without knowing about them first, or discovering them when walking in person. Food trucks would become a more viable option for getting food, more comparable to traditional restaurants, as they can be easily located and ordered from with this app.
